How to Check for Leaks in Your Ductwork: A Homeowner’s Guide

If your energy bills keep climbing or certain rooms in your home never seem to reach a comfortable temperature, leaky ductwork could be the culprit. Ducts are the hidden highways that carry heated or cooled air throughout your home, and even small gaps or cracks can waste a significant amount of energy. In fact, the U.S. Department of Energy estimates that the average home loses about 20-30% of the air moving through its duct system due to leaks, holes, and poor connections. The good news is that with a little know-how, you can spot the warning signs early and know when it's time to call in a professional.

Signs You May Have Duct Leaks

Before grabbing a flashlight and heading into the attic or crawl space, look for these common red flags around your home:

  • Uneven temperatures: Some rooms feel too hot or too cold compared to others, even though your thermostat says otherwise.
  • Rising utility bills: A noticeable spike in your heating or cooling costs without a change in usage habits.
  • Dust buildup: Leaky ducts can pull in dust and debris from attics or crawl spaces and distribute it through your vents.
  • Weak airflow: Vents that used to blow strong air now feel weak or inconsistent.
  • Strange noises: Whistling or hissing sounds coming from your ductwork, especially near joints and seams.

Simple DIY Checks You Can Do Today

While a full inspection is best left to a trained technician, there are a few basic checks homeowners can perform to get a sense of their duct system's condition.

1. Visual Inspection

Head into accessible areas like the attic, basement, or crawl space where ductwork is visible. Look for obvious signs of damage such as disconnected sections, crushed ducts, or visible holes. Pay close attention to the metal tape or mastic sealant at joints—if it's peeling, cracked, or missing, air is likely escaping there.

2. Feel for Airflow

With your HVAC system running, run your hand along the length of exposed ductwork, especially at seams and connections. If you feel air blowing out where it shouldn't be, you've likely found a leak.

3. The Incense Stick Test

Light an incense stick or a thin candle and hold it near duct seams while your system is on. If the smoke wavers or gets pulled toward the duct, that's a sign air is escaping (or being pulled in) at that spot.

4. Check Your Vents

Walk through your home and hold your hand near each supply vent. Weak or inconsistent airflow at multiple vents can indicate leaks somewhere in the system, especially if some rooms have strong airflow and others don't.

Why Professional Duct Testing Matters

DIY checks are a great starting point, but they only catch the leaks you can see or feel. Many duct systems run through walls, ceilings, and other hard-to-reach areas where leaks go undetected for years. HVAC professionals use specialized tools like duct blaster tests and pressure diagnostics to measure exactly how much air your system is losing and pinpoint the precise location of leaks—even in hidden sections of ductwork.

Professional testing not only identifies problems but also helps prioritize repairs based on severity, ensuring you're not spending money fixing minor issues while a major leak continues to drain your energy dollars.

What Happens If Leaks Go Unaddressed?

Ignoring duct leaks doesn't just mean higher energy bills. Over time, leaky ductwork can:

  • Force your HVAC system to work harder, shortening its lifespan
  • Reduce indoor air quality by pulling in dust, allergens, and even pest debris from attics or crawl spaces
  • Create uneven humidity levels, leading to mold growth in certain areas
  • Increase strain on specific rooms, making them uncomfortable year-round

Sealing and Repair Options

Once leaks are identified, they can typically be sealed using mastic sealant or metal-backed tape—never standard duct tape, which breaks down quickly under temperature changes. In cases of significant damage, sections of ductwork may need to be replaced or reinforced. Insulating ducts that run through unconditioned spaces like attics or crawl spaces can also help maintain efficiency after repairs are made.
